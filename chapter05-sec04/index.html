    title: plot title

    """

    if isinstance(surroundings, np.ndarray):

    # base at the origin

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# functions from Chapter 5.3
def transformation_matrix(angle, tx, ty):
    """Homogeneous matrix: rotate by angle (radians), then shift by (tx, ty)."""
    return np.array([
        [np.cos(angle), -np.sin(angle), tx],
        [np.sin(angle),  np.cos(angle), ty],
        [0.0,            0.0,           1.0],
    ])

def transform(T, points):
    """Applies the homogeneous matrix T to all points, row = [x, y]."""
    points_hom = np.ones((len(points), 3))
    points_hom[:, 0:2] = points
    result = (T @ points_hom.T).T
    return result[:, 0:2]

def rotation_about_point(angle, center):
    """Homogeneous matrix: rotation about the point center, angle in radians."""
    T_to_origin = transformation_matrix(0, -center[0], -center[1])
    T_rot = transformation_matrix(angle, 0, 0)
    T_back = transformation_matrix(0, center[0], center[1])
    return T_back @ T_rot @ T_to_origin

Solution Part 1
def robot_position(alpha, beta):
    """Arm points and part corners for the joint angles in radians."""
    T_alpha = transformation_matrix(alpha, 0, 0)
    T_arm2 = T_alpha @ rotation_about_point(beta, arm[1])
    # arm 1 only with T_alpha, arm 2 and the part with both rotations
    arm1_new = transform(T_alpha, arm[0:2])
    arm2_new = transform(T_arm2, arm[1:3])
    arm_new = np.array([arm1_new[0], arm1_new[1], arm2_new[1]])
    part_new = transform(T_arm2, part)
    return arm_new, part_new

At α=9°\alpha = 9°α=9° and β=99°\beta = 99°β=99°, the gripper misses P1P_1P1​ by 3.2 mm3.2\,\text{mm}3.2mm. Unlike the center of the box in Chapter 5.2, however, the gripper can reach P1P_1P1​ exactly: with the exact angles α≈9.4°\alpha \approx 9.4°α≈9.4° and β≈99.0°\beta \approx 99.0°β≈99.0°, it would hit the point with no remaining distance. Here, the remaining 3.2 mm3.2\,\text{mm}3.2mm is therefore purely a discretization error of the 1°1°1° grid. For a placement robot, this is far too inaccurate, because parts have to be placed to within a fraction of a millimeter. A grid ten times finer would help, but it would take much longer. We have already computed 181⋅151=27 331181 \cdot 151 = 27\,331181⋅151=27331 positions. With steps of 0.1°0.1°0.1°, there would be 1801⋅15011801 \cdot 15011801⋅1501, i.e. about 2.7 million positions, a hundred times as many.

Grid search is just the simplest way to solve the inverse kinematics numerically. For an arm with two joints, the angles can even be computed exactly, using the law of cosines in the triangle formed by arm 1, arm 2 and the line from the origin to the target. For more complex robots, such formulas do not always exist. In Chapter 8 we will learn about gradient descent, a numerical method that heads straight for the minimum instead of trying out all combinations.

def search_angles(target):
    """Joint angles in degrees that bring the gripper closest to target."""
    best_distance = 10.0
    for alpha_deg in np.linspace(0, 180, 181):
        for beta_deg in np.linspace(0, 150, 151):
            arm_new, part_new = robot_position(np.deg2rad(alpha_deg), np.deg2rad(beta_deg))
            difference = arm_new[2] - target
            distance = np.sqrt(difference[0]**2 + difference[1]**2)
            if distance < best_distance:
                best_distance = distance
                best_alpha = alpha_deg
                best_beta = beta_deg
    return best_alpha, best_beta, best_distance

All three parts land close to their placement sites and miss them by 3.2 to 3.7 mm3.7\,\text{mm}3.7mm. As in Part 2, this is a discretization error of the grid, and it is too large for placing parts. In addition, the parts lie on the circuit board at different angles. Our robot has no rotary axis at the gripper, so the part is rigidly attached to arm 2. Its long axis therefore always points in the direction of arm 2. Arm 1 is rotated by α\alphaα relative to the xxx-axis, and arm 2 bends by a further β\betaβ. The part is thus always rotated by α+β\alpha + \betaα+β. The angles that the controller chooses therefore determine not only the position but also the orientation of the part.

Closing question

  1. We allowed only angles between 0°0°0° and 150°150°150° for joint 2. Is there a second position that also brings the gripper to P1P_1P1​? What does it look like, and why does the controller commit to one of the two?

  2. Why is the robot, with its two joints, unable to set the position and the orientation of the part independently of each other?

Solution closing question

  1. Yes. Joint 1, joint 2 and the gripper form a triangle with side lengths 0.4 m0.4\,\text{m}0.4m, 0.3 m0.3\,\text{m}0.3m and 0.461 m0.461\,\text{m}0.461m. The third side is the distance from P1P_1P1​ to the origin. This triangle can be mirrored across the line from the origin to P1P_1P1​. In the mirrored position, joint 2 bends clockwise, at roughly α=89°\alpha = 89°α=89° and β=−99°\beta = -99°β=−99°. If the controller allowed both signs of β\betaβ, the search might find one position or the other, depending on the grid. The arm might then swing all the way over between two neighboring sites. That is why the controller of a SCARA robot works with one fixed arm configuration.

  2. The part is always rotated by α+β\alpha + \betaα+β. The placement site already determines α\alphaα and β\betaβ, apart from the mirrored solution, and with them the orientation. A part in the plane, however, has three degrees of freedom: the two coordinates and the angle of rotation. To set all three independently, the robot needs a third rotary axis. Many SCARA robots have it directly at the gripper, where it rotates the part about its own center. With rotation_about_point from Chapter 5.3, we could add this rotation to the chain as a homogeneous matrix as well.

The gripper travels 0.603 m0.603\,\text{m}0.603m, while the straight line is only 0.528 m0.528\,\text{m}0.528m long. The detour is therefore about 14%. We end the straight line at the end point of the path, not at P1P_1P1​, because the grid puts this end point 3.2 mm3.2\,\text{mm}3.2mm away from P1P_1P1​. This way we compare two paths between the same points, and the grid error does not affect the detour.

The controller rotates the joints uniformly but does not move the gripper in a straight line. Because β\betaβ changes eleven times as fast as α\alphaα, it is mainly arm 2 that rotates about joint 2, and the gripper traces an arc around this joint. In robotics, this kind of motion is called a PTP motion, short for point-to-point. It is simple because the controller only needs to know the angles at the start and at the target. If the gripper has to move in a straight line, for example when inserting a part into a hole, the controller must solve the inverse kinematics for many points along the line. In Chapter 6 we will investigate how to move a body along a given path.
