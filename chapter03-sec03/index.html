3.3 Heat Transfer Through a Multilayer Wall

In Chapter 3.1 we solved linear systems of equations with NumPy, and in Chapter 3.2 we computed the support reactions of a beam. Both times we set up the equations by hand. Now we apply the same tool to a classic mechanical engineering problem: an exterior wall made of three layers with different thermal resistances. What is the temperature at the interfaces, and how large is the heat flow?

We will see that the path from the physical equations to the matrix is always the same: all unknowns on the left-hand side, all known quantities on the right.

Learning objectives¶

Learning objectives

  • You can rearrange physical balance equations so that all unknowns are on the left-hand side.

  • You can read off the coefficient matrix A\mathbf{A}A and the vector b⃗\vec{b}b from the rearranged equations.

  • You can solve the linear system with np.linalg.solve and interpret the result physically, including a negative sign.

The physical model¶

We consider a wall made of three layers A, B, C with thermal resistances RAR_ARA​, RBR_BRB​, RCR_CRC​ in K/W. On the left the temperature is TLAT_{LA}TLA​, on the right TCRT_{CR}TCR​.

In the steady state the heat flow QQQ is the same through all layers. For each layer, Fourier’s law of heat conduction holds, written here in resistance form analogous to Ohm’s law:

Q=ΔTiRi,Q = \frac{\Delta T_i}{R_i},Q=Ri​ΔTi​​,

where ΔTi\Delta T_iΔTi​ is the temperature difference across the layer. With the two unknown interface temperatures TABT_{AB}TAB​, TBCT_{BC}TBC​ and the unknown heat flow QQQ, this gives three equations:

TLA−TABRA=Q(1)\frac{T_{LA} - T_{AB}}{R_A} = Q \qquad (1)RA​TLA​−TAB​​=Q(1)
TAB−TBCRB=Q(2)\frac{T_{AB} - T_{BC}}{R_B} = Q \qquad (2)RB​TAB​−TBC​​=Q(2)
TBC−TCRRC=Q(3)\frac{T_{BC} - T_{CR}}{R_C} = Q \qquad (3)RC​TBC​−TCR​​=Q(3)

Solution
R_total = 0.5 + 0.3 + 0.7
Q = (293 - 273) / R_total
print(f'Total resistance: {R_total} K/W')
print(f'Heat flow Q:       {Q:.4f} W')

The heat flow is the same everywhere because in the steady state heat neither accumulates nor disappears anywhere: whatever flows into a layer must also flow back out. If QQQ in layer B were larger than in layer A, heat would continually disappear at the A-B interface, the temperature there would drop, and the state would not be steady. The total resistance is 1.5 K/W, and the heat flow is about 13.33 W. The system of equations gives the same result right away.

From the equations to matrix form¶

The three equations contain the unknowns in fractions. We bring all unknowns to the left-hand side by multiplying each equation by RiR_iRi​ and rearranging. We write each equation so that the term Ri⋅QR_i \cdot QRi​⋅Q has a positive sign:

TAB+RA⋅Q=TLA(1′)T_{AB} + R_A \cdot Q = T_{LA} \qquad (1')TAB​+RA​⋅Q=TLA​(1′)
−TAB+TBC+RB⋅Q=0(2′)-T_{AB} + T_{BC} + R_B \cdot Q = 0 \qquad (2')−TAB​+TBC​+RB​⋅Q=0(2′)
−TBC+RC⋅Q=−TCR(3′)-T_{BC} + R_C \cdot Q = -T_{CR} \qquad (3')−TBC​+RC​⋅Q=−TCR​(3′)

Now we read off the coefficient matrix row by row. The solution vector is x⃗=(TAB, TBC, Q)⊤\vec{x} = (T_{AB},\ T_{BC},\ Q)^\topx=(TAB​, TBC​, Q)⊤:

(+10RA−1+1RB0−1RC)⋅(TABTBCQ)=(TLA0−TCR)\begin{pmatrix} +1 & 0 & R_A \\ -1 & +1 & R_B \\ 0 & -1 & R_C \end{pmatrix} \cdot \begin{pmatrix} T_{AB} \\ T_{BC} \\ Q \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} T_{LA} \\ 0 \\ -T_{CR} \end{pmatrix}⎝⎛​+1−10​0+1−1​RA​RB​RC​​⎠⎞​⋅⎝⎛​TAB​TBC​Q​⎠⎞​=⎝⎛​TLA​0−TCR​​⎠⎞​

Each rearranged equation gives one row of A\mathbf{A}A and one entry in b⃗\vec{b}b. The coefficient of the jjj-th unknown in the iii-th equation sits in AijA_{ij}Aij​. Unknowns that do not appear in an equation get the coefficient 0.

Implementation and solution¶

We set up A\mathbf{A}A and b⃗\vec{b}b in NumPy, check the determinant and solve the system as in Chapter 3.1. With T_AB, T_BC, Q = x we then unpack the solution vector into three variables with meaningful names.

import numpy as np

# given quantities
R_A = 0.5    # thermal resistance layer A in K/W
R_B = 0.3    # thermal resistance layer B in K/W
R_C = 0.7    # thermal resistance layer C in K/W
T_LA = 293.0    # temperature left side (inside) in K
T_CR = 273.0    # temperature right side (outside) in K

# coefficient matrix, unknowns x = [T_AB, T_BC, Q]
A = np.array([
    [+1.0,  0.0, R_A],   # equation (1'):  T_AB + R_A*Q = T_LA
    [-1.0, +1.0, R_B],   # equation (2'): -T_AB + T_BC + R_B*Q = 0
    [ 0.0, -1.0, R_C],   # equation (3'): -T_BC + R_C*Q = -T_CR
])
b = np.array([T_LA, 0.0, -T_CR])

# check solvability
det_A = np.linalg.det(A)
print(f'Determinant: {det_A:.4f}')

# solve
x = np.linalg.solve(A, b)
T_AB, T_BC, Q = x   # unpack the result into three variables

print(f'T_AB = {T_AB:.2f} K   (interface A-B)')
print(f'T_BC = {T_BC:.2f} K   (interface B-C)')
print(f'Q    = {Q:.4f} W   (heat flow)')

print('Check passed:', np.allclose(A @ x, b))
Determinant: 1.5000
T_AB = 286.33 K   (interface A-B)
T_BC = 282.33 K   (interface B-C)
Q    = 13.3333 W   (heat flow)
Check passed: True

The heat flow QQQ is positive. This matches our setup: the positive direction points from left to right, and heat flows from the warmer left side (TLA=293T_{LA} = 293TLA​=293 K) to the colder right side (TCR=273T_{CR} = 273TCR​=273 K). A negative value would mean that the heat flows in the other direction.

As a check, we compute the temperature difference across each layer. Layer C has the largest resistance and should therefore show the largest temperature jump, just as the largest resistance in an electrical circuit produces the largest voltage drop. Unlike in equations (1) to (3), we take the temperature on the right minus the temperature on the left of each layer, so that the three differences add up to TCR−TLAT_{CR} - T_{LA}TCR​−TLA​.

delta_A = T_AB - T_LA
delta_B = T_BC - T_AB
delta_C = T_CR - T_BC

print(f'Temperature difference layer A (R = {R_A} K/W): {delta_A:.2f} K')
print(f'Temperature difference layer B (R = {R_B} K/W): {delta_B:.2f} K')
print(f'Temperature difference layer C (R = {R_C} K/W): {delta_C:.2f} K')
print(f'Sum: {delta_A + delta_B + delta_C:.2f} K '
      f'(must equal T_CR - T_LA = {T_CR - T_LA:.1f} K)')
Temperature difference layer A (R = 0.5 K/W): -6.67 K
Temperature difference layer B (R = 0.3 K/W): -4.00 K
Temperature difference layer C (R = 0.7 K/W): -9.33 K
Sum: -20.00 K (must equal T_CR - T_LA = -20.0 K)

Layer C indeed shows the largest temperature jump at about −9.3 K, and layer B, with the smallest resistance, the smallest at −4.0 K. All differences are negative because the temperature falls from left to right, and together they add up to exactly −20 K.

Solution
import numpy as np

R_A = 0.2
R_B = 1.8
R_C = 0.05
T_LA = 268.0    # inside (cold)
T_CR = 293.0    # outside (warm)

A = np.array([
    [+1.0,  0.0, R_A],
    [-1.0, +1.0, R_B],
    [ 0.0, -1.0, R_C],
])
b = np.array([T_LA, 0.0, -T_CR])

x = np.linalg.solve(A, b)
T_AB, T_BC, Q = x

print(f'T_AB = {T_AB:.2f} K  ({T_AB - 273.15:.1f} °C)')
print(f'T_BC = {T_BC:.2f} K  ({T_BC - 273.15:.1f} °C)')
print(f'Q    = {Q:.4f} W')
print('Check passed:', np.allclose(A @ x, b))

print(f'Temperature drop layer A (concrete): {abs(T_AB - T_LA):.2f} K')
print(f'Temperature drop layer B (foam):     {abs(T_BC - T_AB):.2f} K')
print(f'Temperature drop layer C (steel):    {abs(T_CR - T_BC):.2f} K')

The largest temperature drop occurs in layer B, the polyurethane foam, at about 22 K. Its thermal resistance, RB=1.8R_B = 1.8RB​=1.8 K/W, is by far the largest. That is exactly the point of thermal insulation: it takes on almost the entire temperature difference between inside and outside.

The heat flow is negative (about −12.2 W) because we chose the positive direction from left (inside) to right (outside), but the heat actually flows from the warm outside into the cold storage room. Magnitude and sign together are correct.

Summary and outlook¶

The approach is always the same: rearrange the physical balance equations, move all unknowns to the left, all known quantities to the right, then read off A\mathbf{A}A and b⃗\vec{b}b row by row. np.linalg.solve provides the solution, np.allclose verifies it, and we interpret the sign of the result through the chosen direction convention.
