5.3 Homogeneous Coordinates for a Robot Arm

In Chapter 5.1 we kept joint 2 of the SCARA robot straight, so the gripper could only reach points on a circle of radius 0.7 m0.7\,\text{m}0.7m, just like the swivel arm from Chapter 5.2. To place a part anywhere on the circuit board, joint 2 has to rotate as well. Where is the gripper when joint 1 rotates by 30° and joint 2 by 60°?

The rotation matrix alone cannot answer this question, because it rotates only about the origin. Joint 2, however, sits at the end of arm 1. In this chapter we build a rotation about another point from shifts and a rotation about the origin. Then we append a third coordinate with the value 1 to every point. With these homogeneous coordinates we combine rotation and translation in a single matrix and compute the position of the whole arm.

Learning objectives¶

Learning objectives

  • You can build a rotation about any point from shifting, rotating and shifting back.

  • You can convert points to homogeneous coordinates and build a homogeneous transformation matrix from a rotation and a translation.

  • You can chain several transformations by matrix multiplication and know why the order matters.

def rotation_matrix(angle):
    """Rotation matrix for a rotation about the origin, angle in radians."""
    return np.array([
        [np.cos(angle), -np.sin(angle)],
        [np.sin(angle),  np.cos(angle)],
    ])

So we rotated the gripper as if joint 1 had rotated. How do we rotate about joint 2 instead? We shift the arm so that joint 2 lies at the origin. Then the rotation matrix rotates about the right point. Afterward, we shift everything back:

  1. Shift: we subtract the position of joint 2 from every point.

  2. Rotate: we multiply by the rotation matrix.

  3. Shift back: we add the position of joint 2 again.

joint2 = arm[1]

# step 2: rotate about the origin, where joint 2 now lies
gripper_rotated = R @ gripper_shifted
# step 3: shift back
gripper_new = gripper_rotated + joint2

print('After step 1:', np.round(gripper_shifted, 4))
print('After step 2:', np.round(gripper_rotated, 4))
print('After step 3:', np.round(gripper_new, 4))

We treat the part the same way. If we subtract a vector with two entries from an array of shape (4, 2), NumPy subtracts it from every row. This way we shift all four corners at once. Joint 1 and joint 2 do not move during this rotation, so we copy them unchanged into the new arm.

# joint 1 and joint 2 stay in place, only the gripper moves

Translation as a matrix¶

Our calculation works, but it mixes two kinds of operations: shifting is an addition, rotating is a matrix multiplication. Each additional joint adds three more steps. An industrial robot with six joints would need a long chain of additions and multiplications. It would be more convenient to write each step as a matrix and combine all the matrices into a single one. Can a translation be written as a 2×2 matrix?

Unfortunately not. A matrix M\mathbf{M}M that shifts every point by t⃗=(tx,ty)⊤\vec{t} = (t_x, t_y)^\topt=(tx​,ty​)⊤ would also have to shift the origin, i.e. satisfy M⋅0⃗=t⃗\mathbf{M} \cdot \vec{0} = \vec{t}M⋅0=t. However, the product of a matrix and the zero vector is always the zero vector, because each entry of the product is a sum of multiples of 0. A 2×2 matrix therefore always leaves the origin where it is.

The solution is a trick: we append a third coordinate with the value 1 to every point. (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) becomes (x,y,1)(x, y, 1)(x,y,1). This notation is called homogeneous coordinates. The third coordinate is not a height zzz in space but merely a computational device for translations. For points, it always has the value 1. Now a 3×3 matrix can translate, because the 1 in the third coordinate adds the translation to the result:

(10tx01ty001)(xy1)=(1⋅x+0⋅y+tx⋅10⋅x+1⋅y+ty⋅10⋅x+0⋅y+1⋅1)=(x+txy+ty1).\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & t_x \\ 0 & 1 & t_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \cdot x + 0 \cdot y + t_x \cdot 1 \\ 0 \cdot x + 1 \cdot y + t_y \cdot 1 \\ 0 \cdot x + 0 \cdot y + 1 \cdot 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} x + t_x \\ y + t_y \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}.⎝⎛​100​010​tx​ty​1​⎠⎞​⎝⎛​xy1​⎠⎞​=⎝⎛​1⋅x+0⋅y+tx​⋅10⋅x+1⋅y+ty​⋅10⋅x+0⋅y+1⋅1​⎠⎞​=⎝⎛​x+tx​y+ty​1​⎠⎞​.

If we replace the ones and zeros in the top left with the rotation matrix, we get the homogeneous transformation matrix

T=(cos⁡φ−sin⁡φtxsin⁡φcos⁡φty001)=(R(φ)t⃗0⃗⊤1).\mathbf{T} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\varphi & -\sin\varphi & t_x \\ \sin\varphi & \cos\varphi & t_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} = \left(\begin{array}{c|c} \mathbf{R}(\varphi) & \vec{t} \\ \hline \vec{0}^\top & 1 \end{array}\right).T=⎝⎛​cosφsinφ0​−sinφcosφ0​tx​ty​1​⎠⎞​=(R(φ)0⊤​t1​​).

It first rotates a point about the origin and then shifts it by t⃗\vec{t}t. The last row (0,0,1)(0, 0, 1)(0,0,1) ensures that the third coordinate keeps the value 1 after the transformation. With φ=0\varphi = 0φ=0, T\mathbf{T}T is a pure translation; with t⃗=0⃗\vec{t} = \vec{0}t=0, it is a pure rotation. The pure rotation looks exactly like Rz(φ)\mathbf{R}_z(\varphi)Rz​(φ) from Chapter 5.1. Both matrices leave the third coordinate unchanged, except that there it is the height and here it is the 1 of the homogeneous coordinates.

We check this with steps 2 and 3 from the previous section. After step 1, the gripper is at (0.3,0)(0.3, 0)(0.3,0). A single matrix now rotates by 90°90°90° and shifts back by (0.4,0)(0.4, 0)(0.4,0):

(0−10.4100001)(0.301)=(0⋅0.3+(−1)⋅0+0.4⋅11⋅0.3+0⋅0+0⋅10⋅0.3+0⋅0+1⋅1)=(0.40.31).\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 & 0.4 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0.3 \\ 0 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \cdot 0.3 + (-1) \cdot 0 + 0.4 \cdot 1 \\ 1 \cdot 0.3 + 0 \cdot 0 + 0 \cdot 1 \\ 0 \cdot 0.3 + 0 \cdot 0 + 1 \cdot 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.4 \\ 0.3 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}.⎝⎛​010​−100​0.401​⎠⎞​⎝⎛​0.301​⎠⎞​=⎝⎛​0⋅0.3+(−1)⋅0+0.4⋅11⋅0.3+0⋅0+0⋅10⋅0.3+0⋅0+1⋅1​⎠⎞​=⎝⎛​0.40.31​⎠⎞​.

If we drop the third coordinate, the gripper is at (0.4,0.3)(0.4, 0.3)(0.4,0.3), as expected.

Homogeneous coordinates in robotics

In 3D space, we append a fourth coordinate 1 to (x,y,z)(x, y, z)(x,y,z) and work with 4×4 matrices made up of a 3×3 rotation matrix and a translation vector. Robot controllers describe each joint this way, and the position of the gripper follows from the product of all the matrices.

In Python we write T\mathbf{T}T as a function transformation_matrix(angle, tx, ty).

def transformation_matrix(angle, tx, ty):
    """Homogeneous matrix: rotate by angle (radians), then shift by (tx, ty)."""
    return np.array([
        [np.cos(angle), -np.sin(angle), tx],
        [np.sin(angle),  np.cos(angle), ty],
        [0.0,            0.0,           1.0],
    ])

# step 1 as a matrix: pure translation, joint 2 moves to the origin
T_to_origin = transformation_matrix(0, -0.4, 0)
# steps 2 and 3 in one matrix: rotate by 90 degrees, then shift back
T_rot_back = transformation_matrix(np.deg2rad(90), 0.4, 0)

print('T_rot_back:')
print(np.round(T_rot_back, 4))

To transform many points at once, we use the ready-made helper function transform(T, points). It appends the 1 to every point, multiplies as in Chapter 5.1 with (T @ points_hom.T).T and drops the third coordinate again at the end. The comments explain how it does this step by step.

# Given helper function: just run it, the details are in the comments.
def transform(T, points):
    """Applies the homogeneous matrix T to all points, row = [x, y]."""
    # array of ones: one row per point, three columns
    points_hom = np.ones((len(points), 3))
    # enter x and y into columns 0 and 1 using slicing from Chapter 4.1
    points_hom[:, 0:2] = points
    # transform all points at once as in Chapter 5.1
    result = (T @ points_hom.T).T
    # drop the third coordinate again
    return result[:, 0:2]

Chaining transformations¶

We transformed the part with two matrices, one after the other. Matrix multiplication is associative, so for a point p~=(x,y,1)⊤\tilde{p} = (x, y, 1)^\topp~​=(x,y,1)⊤ in homogeneous coordinates, we can move the parentheses:

T2⋅(T1⋅p~)=(T2⋅T1)⋅p~.\mathbf{T}_2 \cdot (\mathbf{T}_1 \cdot \tilde{p}) = (\mathbf{T}_2 \cdot \mathbf{T}_1) \cdot \tilde{p}.T2​⋅(T1​⋅p~​)=(T2​⋅T1​)⋅p~​.

The product T2⋅T1\mathbf{T}_2 \cdot \mathbf{T}_1T2​⋅T1​ is a single matrix that performs both steps at once. Combining transformations in this way is called composition. The matrix that acts first is on the right, just as with the rotations in the bonus exercise of Chapter 5.2. For a rotation about any point, we chain the three steps in the function rotation_about_point.

def rotation_about_point(angle, center):
    """Homogeneous matrix: rotation about the point center, angle in radians."""
    T_to_origin = transformation_matrix(0, -center[0], -center[1])
    T_rot = transformation_matrix(angle, 0, 0)
    T_back = transformation_matrix(0, center[0], center[1])
    # the matrix applied first is on the right
    return T_back @ T_rot @ T_to_origin

# rotation about joint 2 by 90 degrees as a single matrix
T_joint2 = rotation_about_point(np.deg2rad(90), arm[1])
print(np.round(T_joint2, 4))

This is not the position of joint 2 but exactly the translation that brings joint 2 back to its place after the rotation: the rotation sends (0.4,0)(0.4, 0)(0.4,0) to (0,0.4)(0, 0.4)(0,0.4), and (0,0.4)+(0.4,−0.4)(0, 0.4) + (0.4, -0.4)(0,0.4)+(0.4,−0.4) gives (0.4,0)(0.4, 0)(0.4,0) again.   • We transform the gripper and the part with Tα⋅Tβ\mathbf{T}_\alpha \cdot \mathbf{T}_\betaTα​⋅Tβ​.

# swapped order: first T_alpha, then T_beta
T_wrong = T_beta @ T_alpha
arm2_wrong = transform(T_wrong, arm[1:3])
part_wrong = transform(T_wrong, part)

print('Arm 2 with swapped order in m:')
print(np.round(arm2_wrong, 4))

plot_robot([arm_new, arm2_wrong], parts=[part_new, part_wrong],
           labels=['correct: T_alpha @ T_beta', 'swapped: T_beta @ T_alpha'],
           title='Order swapped')

With the order swapped, arm 2 comes off: its joint ends up at (0.200,0.054)(0.200, 0.054)(0.200,0.054) instead of (0.346,0.200)(0.346, 0.200)(0.346,0.200). The reason is the center of rotation of Tβ\mathbf{T}_\betaTβ​. This matrix rotates about the point (0.4,0)(0.4, 0)(0.4,0), where joint 2 sits in the home position. After Tα\mathbf{T}_\alphaTα​, however, joint 2 is no longer there, and Tβ\mathbf{T}_\betaTβ​ rotates about a point where there is no joint at all. The matrix product is therefore not commutative in general, and when we chain transformations, we have to pay close attention to the order.

Summary and outlook¶

A rotation matrix rotates only about the origin. To rotate about another point, we shift this point to the origin, rotate there and shift back. A translation cannot be written as a 2×2 matrix, because every matrix keeps the origin in place. Homogeneous coordinates solve this problem: with a third coordinate 1, the homogeneous transformation matrix T\mathbf{T}T combines rotation and translation in a 3×3 matrix. We chain several transformations by matrix multiplication, with the matrix applied first on the right. A rotation about any point is thus the composition Tback⋅Trot⋅Tto origin\mathbf{T}_\text{back} \cdot \mathbf{T}_\text{rot} \cdot \mathbf{T}_\text{to origin}Tback​⋅Trot​⋅Tto origin​. In the SCARA robot, arm 2 follows every motion of arm 1. We therefore transform its points with Tα⋅Tβ\mathbf{T}_\alpha \cdot \mathbf{T}_\betaTα​⋅Tβ​, and the points of arm 1 only with Tα\mathbf{T}_\alphaTα​. If we swap the order, joint 2 rotates about a point where it no longer is.

So far we have used the joint angles to compute where the gripper is. In the assembly cell, the opposite question usually arises: a part has to go to a specific spot on the circuit board. Which angles does the controller have to set for this? We explore this question in the project in Chapter 5.4.

In this chapter we rotated and shifted all points within one common coordinate system. In Chapter 6 we will look at a second perspective: there, each body has its own coordinate system, and we convert points between these coordinate systems. This is also how most robot controllers describe their arms.
