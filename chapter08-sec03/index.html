8.3 Location for a Central Warehouse

A supplier delivers to five assembly plants in the region. In the future, a single central warehouse will supply all of them. The plants need different numbers of truckloads per week. Management has proposed a vacant site in the northeast of the region. Where should the central warehouse be located so that the trucks drive the shortest total distance?

In Chapter 8.1, the cost function depended on a single quantity, the order quantity. A location, however, has two coordinates. So we extend gradient descent to two quantities. In doing so, the derivative becomes the gradient, which gives the method its name.

Learning objectives¶

Learning objectives

  • You can set up a cost function of two quantities and display it as a contour plot.

  • You can compute the partial derivatives with the central difference quotient and combine them into the gradient.

  • You can explain why the negative gradient points in the direction of steepest descent.

  • You can apply gradient descent to two quantities and interpret its path in the contour plot.

We place a coordinate system over the region, with xxx pointing east and yyy pointing north, both in kilometers. The table shows where the plants are located and how many truckloads each plant needs per week.

The proposed site is located at (40,30)(40, 30)(40,30). We store the locations of the plants in an array with one row per plant. Column 0 contains xxx, and column 1 contains yyy.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# locations of the plants in km, row = [x, y]
plants = np.array([
    [ 6.0, 24.0],   # plant 1
    [16.0, 30.0],   # plant 2
    [20.0, 12.0],   # plant 3
    [ 8.0,  6.0],   # plant 4
    [44.0, 10.0],   # plant 5
])
# truckloads per week for each plant
loads = np.array([12, 10, 10, 8, 6])

# management's proposal for the central warehouse in km
proposal = np.array([40.0, 30.0])

As the cost, we count the kilometers that all trucks together drive per week. For simplicity, we use the straight-line distance. If the warehouse is at (x,y)(x, y)(x,y), plant iii is di=(x−xi)2+(y−yi)2d_i = \sqrt{(x - x_i)^2 + (y - y_i)^2}di​=(x−xi​)2+(y−yi​)2​ away by the Pythagorean theorem. With nin_ini​ loads per week, this gives the transport effort

E(x,y)=∑i=15ni⋅diE(x, y) = \sum_{i=1}^{5} n_i \cdot d_iE(x,y)=i=1∑5​ni​⋅di​

in truck-kilometers per week. We only count the trips to the plants. The return trips double the effort but do not change the best location.

The function effort() receives the location as an array [x, y]. If we subtract it from plants, NumPy subtracts it from each row. This gives us the coordinate differences for all five plants at once.

def effort(location):
    """Transport effort in truck-km per week for a warehouse at [x, y]."""
    difference = plants - location
    # straight-line distance to each plant (Pythagorean theorem)
    distances = np.sqrt(difference[:, 0]**2 + difference[:, 1]**2)
    return np.sum(loads * distances)

effort_proposal = effort(proposal)
print(f'Effort at the proposal: {effort_proposal:.1f} truck-km per week')
Effort at the proposal: 1365.8 truck-km per week

With management’s proposal, the trucks drive 1365.8 km1365.8\,\text{km}1365.8km per week. Can we do better? In Chapter 8.1, we plotted the cost against the order quantity. Here, the effort depends on two quantities, so it forms a landscape of hills and valleys over the map. As on a topographic map, we draw this landscape with contour lines, i.e. lines along which the effort is constant.

To do this, we evaluate effort() on a grid with one point every 0.5 km0.5\,\text{km}0.5km. We compute the effort for each value of yyy and each value of xxx in two nested loops and store the result in a matrix. Note that the rows of the matrix correspond to yyy and the columns to xxx, just like on a map.

# grid over the region: x from 0 to 50 km, y from 0 to 35 km
x_values = np.linspace(0, 50, 101)
y_values = np.linspace(0, 35, 71)

# effort at each grid point, row = y, column = x
effort_values = np.zeros((len(y_values), len(x_values)))
for i in range(len(y_values)):
    for j in range(len(x_values)):
        location = np.array([x_values[j], y_values[i]])
        effort_values[i, j] = effort(location)

The contour lines enclose a valley left of center, between plants 1 to 4. This is where the effort is smallest, below 650 km650\,\text{km}650km per week. Toward management’s proposal, the lines get closer and closer together, so the effort rises more and more steeply there. The valley is not in the middle of the map. Plants 1 to 4, with 40 loads in total, pull it to the left, while plant 5, with only 6 loads, barely pulls it to the right.

What is a contour plot?

A contour plot shows a function of two quantities in the plane. Each line connects points with the same function value. Where the lines are close together, the function changes rapidly. If closed lines shrink around a point and the values decrease toward the center, there is a minimum inside them.

Where does the gradient point?¶

As in Chapter 8.1, we want to walk downhill from the proposal. Now, however, we can move in any direction in the plane. So we look at the two coordinate directions separately.

  • How does the effort change if we only change xxx and keep yyy fixed?

  • How does the effort change if we only change yyy and keep xxx fixed?

These two slopes are called partial derivatives, written ∂E/∂x\partial E / \partial x∂E/∂x and ∂E/∂y\partial E / \partial y∂E/∂y. As in Chapter 8.1, we compute each of them with the central difference quotient. To do this, we shift the location by dx either only in the xxx-direction or only in the yyy-direction. Together, the two partial derivatives form a vector, the gradient

∇E=(∂E/∂x∂E/∂y).\nabla E = \begin{pmatrix} \partial E / \partial x \\ \partial E / \partial y \end{pmatrix}.∇E=(∂E/∂x∂E/∂y​).
def gradient(function, point, dx):
    """Gradient of function at point = [x, y].

    Each partial derivative with the central difference quotient.
    """
    shift_x = np.array([dx, 0.0])
    shift_y = np.array([0.0, dx])
    derivative_x = (function(point + shift_x)
                    - function(point - shift_x)) / (2 * dx)
    derivative_y = (function(point + shift_y)
                    - function(point - shift_y)) / (2 * dx)
    return np.array([derivative_x, derivative_y])

At the proposed site, we get the following gradient. np.linalg.norm() computes its length from the two components using the Pythagorean theorem.

# gradient at management's proposal
gradient_proposal = gradient(effort, proposal, 1e-6)
length = np.linalg.norm(gradient_proposal)
print('Gradient at the proposal:', np.round(gradient_proposal, 2))
print(f'Length of the gradient: {length:.2f}')
Gradient at the proposal: [34.47 19.46]
Length of the gradient: 39.59

The first component is the slope in the xxx-direction, i.e. to the east. So if the warehouse moves one kilometer east of the proposed site, the trucks drive about 34.5 km34.5\,\text{km}34.5km more per week. The second component belongs to the yyy-direction: one kilometer north, they drive about 19.5 km19.5\,\text{km}19.5km more. These are approximations, because the gradient describes the change right at the proposal. Both components are positive, so we should move west and south. The gradient points in the direction in which the effort increases fastest. Its length of 39.6 is the slope in exactly this direction. The negative gradient therefore points in the direction of steepest descent. We draw it as an arrow in the contour plot, using ax.annotate() as for the force arrows in Chapter 4. To make the arrow fit on the map, we shorten it to a quarter of its length.

# contour plot with the negative gradient at the proposal
arrow_tip = proposal - 0.25 * gradient_proposal

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 6))
lines = ax.contour(x_values, y_values, effort_values,
                   levels=np.arange(650, 1500, 50))
ax.clabel(lines, fontsize=8)
ax.scatter(plants[:, 0], plants[:, 1], color='black', zorder=3)
ax.scatter(proposal[0], proposal[1], marker='x', s=80, color='red')
ax.annotate('', xy=arrow_tip, xytext=proposal,
            arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle='-|>', color='red', lw=2))
ax.set_aspect('equal')
ax.set_xlabel('x in km')
ax.set_ylabel('y in km')

The arrow is perpendicular to the contour lines. Why? Along a contour line, the effort does not change. So it changes fastest at right angles to the line. A hiker looking for the most direct way down into the valley also walks across the contour lines.

Where is the best location for the central warehouse?¶

We can use gradient descent from Chapter 8.1 almost unchanged. The point is now an array [x, y]. Instead of central(), we call gradient(), and NumPy evaluates the update rule

(xy)new=(xy)−α⋅∇E\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix}_\text{new} = \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} - \alpha \cdot \nabla E(xy​)new​=(xy​)−α⋅∇E

for both coordinates at once. As the stopping criterion, we check the length of the gradient. At the minimum, the gradient is zero, and its length is small only if both components are small.

def gradient_descent_2d(function, start, learning_rate, tolerance,
                        max_iterations):
    """Search for a minimum of function, starting at start = [x, y].

    Returns all visited points as an array, row = [x, y].
    """
    point = start
    history = [point]
    for k in range(max_iterations):
        slope = gradient(function, point, 1e-6)
        # stopping criterion: length of the gradient almost zero
        if np.linalg.norm(slope) < tolerance:
            break
        point = point - learning_rate * slope
        history.append(point)
    return np.array(history)

We start at the proposed site, with a learning rate of 0.2 and a tolerance of 0.01.

# gradient descent starting from the proposal
history = gradient_descent_2d(effort, proposal, 0.2, 0.01, 200)

# result: iterations, location and effort
n_iterations = len(history) - 1
location_best = history[-1]
effort_best = effort(location_best)
print(f'{n_iterations} iterations')
print('Best location in km:', np.round(location_best, 2))
print(f'Effort: {effort_best:.1f} truck-km per week')

From logistics: the center-of-gravity method

A common rule of thumb places the warehouse at the weighted center of gravity of the plants. Its xxx-coordinate is ∑nixi/∑ni\sum n_i x_i / \sum n_i∑ni​xi​/∑ni​, and the yyy-coordinate is computed the same way. However, the center of gravity does not minimize the sum of the distances, but the sum of the squared distances. For the minimum of the sum of the distances, there is in general no closed-form formula. This problem is known as the Weber problem, after the economist Alfred Weber, and it is solved numerically, for example with gradient descent.

At each plant, the effort has a sharp corner where the gradient does not exist. If one plant needs at least half of all loads, the minimum lies exactly at this plant, and gradient descent finds it only approximately. In our example, the minimum lies between the plants, where the gradient is zero.

Summary and outlook¶

If a cost function depends on two quantities, we display it as a contour plot. The partial derivatives tell us how much it changes if we change only one of the two quantities. As in Chapter 8.1, we compute them with the central difference quotient. Together, they form the gradient. It points in the direction of steepest ascent and is perpendicular to the contour lines. Gradient descent moves in the direction of the negative gradient and stops as soon as the length of the gradient is almost zero. In 24 iterations, it found a warehouse location at which the trucks drive about 750 km750\,\text{km}750km less per week than with management’s proposal.

In Chapter 8.4, you will use gradient_descent_2d() to fit a straight line with two parameters to the measured data of a sensor. Unlike here, the two quantities will have different units. We will see that this makes choosing the learning rate harder.
