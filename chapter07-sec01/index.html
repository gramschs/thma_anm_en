7.1 Velocity from Position Data

In Chapter 6.3 we computed the direction of a path from two neighboring path points and saw that the secant only approximates the tangent. Now the AGV starts off in a straight line along the main aisle of the warehouse. We know its position only at individual times, as a sequence of numbers and not as a formula. How fast was the AGV at each of these times?

The velocity is the derivative of the distance with respect to time. Without a formula, we cannot differentiate the way we do in math class. So we approximate the derivative from the measured values. This approximation is called numerical differentiation.

Learning objectives¶

Learning objectives

  • You can compute the average velocity between two measurements as a difference quotient and interpret it as the slope of a secant.

  • You know the forward, the backward and the central difference quotient and know which point in time each result fits.

  • You can use a function to compute the derivative at every point of a measurement series, including the endpoints.

  • You can explain why differentiation amplifies measurement noise.

How fast was the AGV between two reports?¶

The AGV is waiting at the start of the main aisle and drives off in a straight line. Every half second, it reports to the fleet manager how far it has traveled. For now, we assume that these reports are exact. The following applet shows the AGV starting off in slow motion. With “To next report”, the AGV drives on until the next report and stops there. In the fleet manager’s view, the AGV disappears, and only what the fleet manager receives remains.

The times of the reports are stored in the array t, the distances traveled in the array s. We call the time between two reports the step size Δt\Delta tΔt, or dt in the code.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# step size: time between two reports in s
dt = 0.5
# times of the reports in s
t = np.array([0.0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, 3.0, 3.5, 4.0])
# distance traveled by the AGV in m
s = np.array([0.0, 0.05, 0.2, 0.45, 0.8, 1.2, 1.6, 2.0, 2.4])

A plot shows how the distance grows over time.

# plot: distance over time
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 4))
ax.plot(t, s, marker='o')
ax.set_xlabel('Time in s')
ax.set_ylabel('Distance in m')
ax.set_title('AGV starting off in the main aisle')
ax.grid(True)
plt.show()

At first, the graph gets steeper and steeper, and after that it is a straight line. This suggests that the AGV first speeds up and then continues at a constant speed. The marks in the applet show the same thing: the gaps between them first grow and then stay the same. To find out exactly how fast, we compute it from the measured values. Between two reports, the AGV covers the distance sk+1−sks_{k+1} - s_ksk+1​−sk​ in the time Δt\Delta tΔt. The quotient is the average velocity in this time interval:

vˉk=sk+1−skΔt.\bar{v}_k = \frac{s_{k+1} - s_k}{\Delta t}.vˉk​=Δtsk+1​−sk​​.

We compute all values in a loop and collect them in an array that we create beforehand with np.zeros(). Nine reports mark off eight time intervals.

# average velocity in the time interval from t[k] to t[k + 1]
v_avg = np.zeros(len(s) - 1)
for k in range(len(s) - 1):
    v_avg[k] = (s[k + 1] - s[k]) / dt
print('Average velocities in m/s:', v_avg)
Average velocities in m/s: [0.1 0.3 0.5 0.7 0.8 0.8 0.8 0.8]

In the first interval, the AGV moves at only 0.1 m/s0.1\,\text{m/s}0.1m/s on average. From one interval to the next, the average velocity then increases by 0.2 m/s0.2\,\text{m/s}0.2m/s each time, to 0.3 m/s0.3\,\text{m/s}0.3m/s, 0.5 m/s0.5\,\text{m/s}0.5m/s and 0.7 m/s0.7\,\text{m/s}0.7m/s. From the fifth interval on, it stays at 0.8 m/s0.8\,\text{m/s}0.8m/s. So the measured values fit an AGV that accelerates uniformly and then moves at constant velocity. They do not tell us what exactly happens between two reports.

ax.plot() connected the data points with line segments. Each of these segments is a secant, and its slope is exactly the average velocity. A quotient of two differences such as (sk+1−sk)/Δt(s_{k+1} - s_k) / \Delta t(sk+1​−sk​)/Δt is called a difference quotient.

Math refresher: derivative

As Δt\Delta tΔt approaches zero, the secant becomes the tangent and the difference quotient becomes the derivative s′(t)s'(t)s′(t), i.e. the instantaneous velocity v(t)v(t)v(t). Measured values, however, are only available at intervals of Δt\Delta tΔt. That is why the difference quotient remains an approximation. You can review the basics in the Preparation.

Which point in time does the value belong to?¶

So far, each value belongs to a time interval. For a plot of the velocity, however, we need one value per report. There are three ways to get one. At the time tkt_ktk​,

  • the forward difference quotient uses the next report: vk≈(sk+1−sk)/Δtv_k \approx (s_{k+1} - s_k) / \Delta tvk​≈(sk+1​−sk​)/Δt,

  • the backward difference quotient uses the previous report: vk≈(sk−sk−1)/Δtv_k \approx (s_k - s_{k-1}) / \Delta tvk​≈(sk​−sk−1​)/Δt,

  • the central difference quotient uses both neighbors: vk≈(sk+1−sk−1)/(2Δt)v_k \approx (s_{k+1} - s_{k-1}) / (2 \Delta t)vk​≈(sk+1​−sk−1​)/(2Δt).

We try all three at t2=1 st_2 = 1\,\text{s}t2​=1s. For comparison, we reveal how the fleet manager planned the drive: the AGV accelerates uniformly at 0.4 m/s20.4\,\text{m/s}^20.4m/s2 for 2 s2\,\text{s}2s and then continues at 0.8 m/s0.8\,\text{m/s}0.8m/s. So at t=1 st = 1\,\text{s}t=1s, it is moving at exactly 0.4 m/s0.4\,\text{m/s}0.4m/s.

# three difference quotients at the time t[2] = 1 s
k = 2
v_forward = (s[k + 1] - s[k]) / dt
v_backward = (s[k] - s[k - 1]) / dt
v_central = (s[k + 1] - s[k - 1]) / (2 * dt)

# output of the difference quotients
print(f'Forward:  {v_forward:.2f} m/s')
print(f'Backward: {v_backward:.2f} m/s')
print(f'Central:  {v_central:.2f} m/s')
print('Planned:  0.40 m/s')
Forward:  0.50 m/s
Backward: 0.30 m/s
Central:  0.40 m/s
Planned:  0.40 m/s

The forward difference quotient is 0.1 m/s0.1\,\text{m/s}0.1m/s too high, the backward difference quotient 0.1 m/s0.1\,\text{m/s}0.1m/s too low. Why? The forward difference quotient is the average velocity from 1.0 s1.0\,\text{s}1.0s to 1.5 s1.5\,\text{s}1.5s. With uniform acceleration, the AGV reaches this average velocity at the midpoint of the interval, i.e. at 1.25 s1.25\,\text{s}1.25s, half a step too late. In the same way, the backward difference quotient belongs to 0.75 s0.75\,\text{s}0.75s. The central difference quotient is the mean of the two, so their errors cancel out. It fits the time tkt_ktk​ itself.

We can only form the central difference quotient in the interior of the measurement series. The first point has no predecessor, and the last point has no successor. There we fall back on the forward and the backward difference quotient. Since we will need this calculation again, we write it as a function derivative(values, dt).

def derivative(values, dt):
    """Derivative of a measurement series with step size dt.

    Central difference quotient at the interior points, forward at the
    first point, backward at the last point.
    """
    n = len(values)
    result = np.zeros(n)

    # endpoints: one-sided difference quotients
    result[0] = (values[1] - values[0]) / dt
    result[-1] = (values[-1] - values[-2]) / dt

    # interior points: central difference quotient
    for k in range(1, n - 1):
        result[k] = (values[k + 1] - values[k - 1]) / (2 * dt)
    return result

With derivative(), we now compute the velocity at every report and compare it with the fleet manager’s plan.

# velocity planned by the fleet manager, for comparison
v_plan = np.array([0.0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, 0.8, 0.8, 0.8, 0.8])

# velocity of the AGV
v = derivative(s, dt)

# output: computed and planned velocity
print('Computed in m/s:', v)
print('Planned in m/s: ', v_plan)
Computed in m/s: [0.1  0.2  0.4  0.6  0.75 0.8  0.8  0.8  0.8 ]
Planned in m/s:  [0.  0.2 0.4 0.6 0.8 0.8 0.8 0.8 0.8]

# plot: computed and planned velocity

At seven of the nine times, the computed velocity matches the planned one. At the start, the forward difference quotient gives 0.1 m/s0.1\,\text{m/s}0.1m/s instead of 0. We already know this error of half a step. At t=2 st = 2\,\text{s}t=2s, the acceleration stops abruptly, and the graph of the velocity has a kink there. The central difference quotient averages over the interval from 1.5 s1.5\,\text{s}1.5s to 2.5 s2.5\,\text{s}2.5s and gives 0.75 m/s0.75\,\text{m/s}0.75m/s instead of 0.8 m/s0.8\,\text{m/s}0.8m/s. So at kinks, it is off as well.

NumPy provides the same calculation as the function np.gradient(values, dt). Because we wrote our own function derivative(), we know what goes on inside it.

What does measurement noise do?¶

So far, the positions matched the planned motion exactly. However, the AGV keeps determining its position itself, for example from the measurements of its scanner, and each of these position estimates is off by a few millimeters. This random fluctuation of the measured values is called measurement noise. We now evaluate the log that the AGV recorded ten times per second during the same drive.

# AGV log: step size in s and times 0.0, 0.1, ..., 4.0 s
dt_meas = 0.1
t_meas = np.linspace(0, 4, 41)
# measured distance in m, rounded to millimeters
s_meas = np.array([
    0.000, 0.003, 0.007, 0.014, 0.030, 0.046, 0.072, 0.103, 0.126, 0.160,
    0.202, 0.243, 0.288, 0.334, 0.392, 0.453, 0.507, 0.576, 0.640, 0.717,
    0.793, 0.879, 0.955, 1.041, 1.121, 1.199, 1.270, 1.358, 1.440, 1.520,
    1.594, 1.678, 1.756, 1.837, 1.924, 1.997, 2.080, 2.164, 2.238, 2.320,
    2.400,
])

From the positions, we compute the velocity with derivative(). If we apply the function to the velocity, we get the acceleration aaa, the derivative of the velocity with respect to time. The plan was 0.4 m/s20.4\,\text{m/s}^20.4m/s2 until t=2 st = 2\,\text{s}t=2s and 0 m/s20\,\text{m/s}^20m/s2 after that.

# velocity: differentiate once, acceleration: differentiate again
v_meas = derivative(s_meas, dt_meas)
a_meas = derivative(v_meas, dt_meas)
print(f'Acceleration from {np.min(a_meas):.2f} to {np.max(a_meas):.2f} m/s²')
Acceleration from -0.43 to 0.70 m/s²

The velocity follows the plan but jitters by a few centimeters per second. The acceleration is barely recognizable. Why does differentiation amplify the small measurement errors so much?

Suppose a single position sks_ksk​ is 5 mm5\,\text{mm}5mm too large. It appears in the central difference quotients of the two neighboring points, with a plus sign at the point before and with a minus sign at the point after:

vk−1=sk−sk−22Δt,vk+1=sk+2−sk2Δt.v_{k-1} = \frac{s_k - s_{k-2}}{2 \Delta t}, \qquad v_{k+1} = \frac{s_{k+2} - s_k}{2 \Delta t}.vk−1​=2Δtsk​−sk−2​​,vk+1​=2Δtsk+2​−sk​​.

Divided by 2Δt=0.2 s2 \Delta t = 0.2\,\text{s}2Δt=0.2s, this makes the velocity at the point before 0.025 m/s0.025\,\text{m/s}0.025m/s too large and at the point after 0.025 m/s0.025\,\text{m/s}0.025m/s too small. When we differentiate a second time, we take exactly the difference of these two velocities at point kkk. Because of the opposite signs, the errors add up to 0.05 m/s0.05\,\text{m/s}0.05m/s. Divided by 0.2 s0.2\,\text{s}0.2s, this gives an error of 0.25 m/s20.25\,\text{m/s}^20.25m/s2, more than half the planned acceleration. Since we divide by 2Δt2 \Delta t2Δt every time we differentiate, the smaller the step size, the more the error grows. So a small step size is not always better.

Summary and outlook¶

From measured values at intervals of Δt\Delta tΔt, we approximate the derivative with difference quotients. The forward and the backward difference quotient approximately fit the midpoint of an interval, the central difference quotient fits the measurement time itself. With uniform acceleration, this even holds exactly. Our function derivative() uses the central difference quotient at the interior points and the one-sided difference quotients at the endpoints. NumPy offers the same calculation as np.gradient(). Because of the small step size, differentiation amplifies measurement noise, and differentiating twice amplifies it even more.

In Chapter 7.2, the motion of the AGV is given as a formula. Then we can choose the step size freely. We investigate how fast the error of the difference quotients decreases as the step size gets smaller, and why it grows again for very small step sizes.
