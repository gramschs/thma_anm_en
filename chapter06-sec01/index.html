6.1 Vehicle and World Coordinates

In a warehouse, an automated guided vehicle, or AGV for short, brings boxes to a transfer station. Several such vehicles together with their control system form an automated guided vehicle system. The AGV uses a laser scanner to find its way. When the scanner reports an obstacle 1.5 m1.5\,\text{m}1.5m ahead, the controller only knows where the obstacle is relative to the vehicle. Where is the obstacle in the warehouse?

In Chapter 5.3 we rotated and shifted points within one common coordinate system. Now the warehouse and the vehicle each have their own coordinate system, and the same matrix converts coordinates from one to the other.

Learning objectives¶

Learning objectives

  • You know what a world coordinate system and a vehicle coordinate system are, and you can describe the location of a vehicle with its pose.

  • You can build the transformation matrix TWV\mathbf{T}_{WV}TWV​ and use it to convert points from the vehicle coordinate system to the world coordinate system.

  • You can convert measurements given as distance and angle into vehicle coordinates.

  • You can use the inverse matrix TWV−1\mathbf{T}_{WV}^{-1}TWV−1​ to convert points from the world coordinate system to the vehicle coordinate system.

Where is the vehicle in the warehouse?¶

As with the SCARA robot, we look at the warehouse from above and need two coordinate systems:

  • The world coordinate system W is fixed to the warehouse. Its origin is in the lower left corner of our section of the warehouse. The warehouse map is given in this coordinate system.

  • The vehicle coordinate system V moves with the vehicle. Its origin is at the center of the vehicle, the xVx_VxV​-axis points forward and the yVy_VyV​-axis to the left, as specified in the standard ISO 8855.

Together, the position (x,y)(x, y)(x,y) of the vehicle center and the yaw angle ψ\psiψ (psi) between the xWx_WxW​-axis and the xVx_VxV​-axis are called the pose of the vehicle. As in Chapter 5.1, positive yaw angles rotate counterclockwise.

First we run two cells: the given plotting function plot_warehouse and a cell with transformation_matrix and transform from Chapter 5.3 and the warehouse map. It also contains the outline of the AGV, which is 1.2 m1.2\,\text{m}1.2m long and 0.8 m0.8\,\text{m}0.8m wide. In vehicle coordinates, its corners are always in the same places, and the first two form the front edge.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# functions from Chapter 5.3
def transformation_matrix(angle, tx, ty):
    """Homogeneous matrix: rotate by angle (radians), then shift by (tx, ty)."""
    return np.array([
        [np.cos(angle), -np.sin(angle), tx],
        [np.sin(angle),  np.cos(angle), ty],
        [0.0,            0.0,           1.0],
    ])

def transform(T, points):
    """Applies the homogeneous matrix T to all points, row = [x, y]."""
    points_hom = np.ones((len(points), 3))
    points_hom[:, 0:2] = points
    result = (T @ points_hom.T).T
    return result[:, 0:2]

    [ 0.6,  0.4],   # front left
    [-0.6,  0.4],   # rear left
    [-0.6, -0.4],   # rear right
])

We move the vehicle into its pose the same way as the part in Chapter 5.3: first rotate by ψ\psiψ, then shift by (x,y)(x, y)(x,y). The homogeneous transformation matrix does exactly this:

TWV=(cos⁡ψ−sin⁡ψxsin⁡ψcos⁡ψy001).\mathbf{T}_{WV} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\psi & -\sin\psi & x \\ \sin\psi & \cos\psi & y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.TWV​=⎝⎛​cosψsinψ0​−sinψcosψ0​xy1​⎠⎞​.
Subscript notation

We read the subscripts from right to left: TWV\mathbf{T}_{WV}TWV​ converts coordinates from system V to system W. For a point in homogeneous coordinates from Chapter 5.3, we have p~W=TWV⋅p~V\tilde{p}_W = \mathbf{T}_{WV} \cdot \tilde{p}_Vp~​W​=TWV​⋅p~​V​.

Our AGV is at (4,2)(4, 2)(4,2) with ψ=30°\psi = 30°ψ=30°. The parameter coordinate_systems additionally draws the axes of the vehicle coordinate system.

The red axis xVx_VxV​ points forward, the blue axis yVy_VyV​ to the left. Where do we find these axes in the matrix? The first column of TWV\mathbf{T}_{WV}TWV​, (0.866,0.5)(0.866, 0.5)(0.866,0.5), is the direction of the xVx_VxV​-axis in warehouse coordinates, the second column is the direction of the yVy_VyV​-axis, and the third is the vehicle center (4,2)(4, 2)(4,2). We can therefore read TWV\mathbf{T}_{WV}TWV​ in two ways: as a motion that brings the vehicle from the origin into its pose, like the robot arm in Chapter 5.3, or as a conversion of the same corners from vehicle to warehouse coordinates. Both views give the same numbers.

Where is the obstacle in the warehouse?¶

The scanner sits at the center of the vehicle, i.e. at the origin of the vehicle coordinate system. For each laser beam, it measures the distance rrr to the first object and gives the direction as the angle φ\varphiφ measured from the xVx_VxV​-axis, positive to the left, as in the figure above.

Math refresher: polar coordinates

A point at the distance rrr from the origin and at the angle φ\varphiφ measured from the xxx-axis has the polar coordinates (r,φ)(r, \varphi)(r,φ) and the Cartesian coordinates x=rcos⁡φx = r \cos\varphix=rcosφ, y=rsin⁡φy = r \sin\varphiy=rsinφ. This is how we computed the gripper on its circle in Chapter 5.1.

The obstacle from the beginning has r=1.5r = 1.5r=1.5 and φ=0\varphi = 0φ=0, so its vehicle coordinates are (1.5,0)(1.5, 0)(1.5,0). transform expects one point per row, so we write the single point with double square brackets as an array of shape (1, 2).

# obstacle 1.5 m ahead: r = 1.5 m and phi = 0, i.e. (1.5, 0) in vehicle coordinates

Where is the transfer station from the vehicle’s point of view?¶

For the drive to the transfer station at (8,2.5)(8, 2.5)(8,2.5), the controller needs the opposite direction. The vehicle coordinates of the station tell us whether it is in front (xV>0x_V > 0xV​>0) or behind, and whether the AGV has to steer left (yV>0y_V > 0yV​>0) or right. Which matrix converts from W to V?

Math refresher: inverse matrix

The inverse matrix A−1\mathbf{A}^{-1}A−1 undoes the effect of A\mathbf{A}A, i.e. A−1A=I\mathbf{A}^{-1} \mathbf{A} = \mathbf{I}A−1A=I. It exists if det⁡(A)≠0\det(\mathbf{A}) \neq 0det(A)=0. For rotation matrices, it is the transpose (Chapter 5.1). Like the rotation matrix, our homogeneous matrices have the determinant 1 and can always be inverted.

So TVW=TWV−1\mathbf{T}_{VW} = \mathbf{T}_{WV}^{-1}TVW​=TWV−1​ converts from W to V. NumPy computes the inverse with np.linalg.inv. As a check, TVW⋅TWV\mathbf{T}_{VW} \cdot \mathbf{T}_{WV}TVW​⋅TWV​ must give the identity matrix np.eye(3) from Chapter 5.1.

# transfer station in warehouse coordinates in m, as an array of shape (1, 2)
station = np.array([[8.0, 2.5]])

# from warehouse to vehicle coordinates: with the inverse matrix
T_VW = np.linalg.inv(T_WV)
print('T_VW:')
print(np.round(T_VW, 4))
print('T_VW @ T_WV equals the identity matrix:', np.allclose(T_VW @ T_WV, np.eye(3)))

station_V = transform(T_VW, station)
print('Station in vehicle coordinates in m:', np.round(station_V, 3))

plot_warehouse(outline_W, coordinate_systems=T_WV, targets=station,
               surroundings=warehouse, title='Where is the transfer station?')

The station is 3.714 m3.714\,\text{m}3.714m ahead of the vehicle center and 1.567 m1.567\,\text{m}1.567m to the right of it, so the AGV has to steer right. The top left of TVW\mathbf{T}_{VW}TVW​ contains the reverse rotation R(−30°)\mathbf{R}(-30°)R(−30°). The right column, however, is not (−4,−2)(-4, -2)(−4,−2): TWV\mathbf{T}_{WV}TWV​ first rotated and then shifted, so the inverse has to shift back first and then rotate back. In doing so, the reverse rotation also rotates the translation vector.

Summary and outlook¶

The warehouse and the vehicle each have their own coordinate system. The pose (x,y,ψ)(x, y, \psi)(x,y,ψ) determines where the vehicle coordinate system is and where it points. TWV\mathbf{T}_{WV}TWV​ converts vehicle coordinates to warehouse coordinates, and its columns contain the vehicle axes and the vehicle center. We convert scanner measurements to vehicle coordinates with x=rcos⁡φx = r\cos\varphix=rcosφ and y=rsin⁡φy = r\sin\varphiy=rsinφ. The opposite direction is handled by TVW=TWV−1\mathbf{T}_{VW} = \mathbf{T}_{WV}^{-1}TVW​=TWV−1​, which we compute with np.linalg.inv.

In the project in Chapter 6.2, a second scanner sits at the front of the vehicle and brings a third coordinate system with it. As in Chapter 5.3, we therefore chain two transformations and check whether the AGV has to stop.
