2.4 Test-rig Report for an Electric Motor

In chapters 2.1 to 2.3 we computed NumPy arrays, analyzed them statistically and plotted them with Matplotlib. In this chapter we apply these tools together and analyze a complete test-rig run of the electric motor from chapter 2.3. Work through the sub-tasks in pairs if possible and in order, because each part builds on the results of the previous one.

Project: Test-rig report for the electric motor (✩✩)

On the test rig, the rotational speed of the motor was increased in twelve steps from 250 to 3000 1/min. At each step, the torque at the shaft and the electrical power input were measured. The measured values are already stored as arrays in the first code cell. From them we create the figures and key figures for the test-rig report.

Part 1: Read in the measurement data and compute characteristic quantities

In the code cell, the measured values are already stored as arrays rpm, torque and power_input.

  1. Check with .shape that all three arrays are the same length.

  2. Compute the angular velocity omega = 2 * np.pi * rpm / 60 in rad/s.

  3. Compute the mechanical power p_mech = torque * omega in watts.

  4. Compute the efficiency efficiency = p_mech / power_input and print the array.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import matplotlib.style as style
style.use('seaborn-v0_8')

rpm = np.array([250, 500, 750, 1000, 1250, 1500,
                1750, 2000, 2250, 2500, 2750, 3000])           # 1/min
torque = np.array([7.4, 6.7, 6.1, 5.4, 4.7, 4.1,
                   3.4, 2.9, 2.3, 1.7, 1.1, 0.5])              # Nm
power_input = np.array([1350, 1660, 1720, 1520, 1230, 980,
                        800, 720, 660, 600, 560, 470])         # W

# Add: check shapes, compute omega, p_mech and efficiency
Solution Part 1
print(rpm.shape, torque.shape, power_input.shape)

omega = 2 * np.pi * rpm / 60
p_mech = torque * omega
efficiency = p_mech / power_input

print(efficiency)

All three arrays have the shape (12,). The three calculations are vector operations and act on the whole array. The efficiency lies between about 0.14 and 0.84 and is thus everywhere smaller than 1, as it must be for a real motor. It first rises with the rotational speed, reaches a maximum and then falls again.

Part 2: Characteristic map as subplots

Create a Figure with three subplots one below the other (nrows=3). Show the torque at the top, the mechanical power in the middle and the efficiency at the bottom, each over the rotational speed. Label each subplot with its y-axis and give the efficiency subplot the full scale with set_ylim(0, 1). Set the x-axis label and the grid via a for loop over ax. The top subplot gets a title. Align the Figure with plt.tight_layout().

# code cell
Solution Part 2
fig, ax = plt.subplots(nrows=3, ncols=1, figsize=(7, 8))

ax[0].plot(rpm, torque)
ax[0].set_ylabel('Torque in Nm')

ax[1].plot(rpm, p_mech)
ax[1].set_ylabel('Mechanical power in W')

ax[2].plot(rpm, efficiency)
ax[2].set_ylabel('Efficiency')
ax[2].set_ylim(0, 1)

for single_axis in ax:
    single_axis.set_xlabel('Rotational speed in 1/min')
    single_axis.grid(True)

ax[0].set_title('Characteristic map from the test-rig run')

plt.tight_layout()
plt.show()

This is the first time we see the three measured quantities in context. We treat the measured points as a characteristic curve and connect them with a line to show the trend. The torque falls approximately linearly, the mechanical power has its maximum in the middle of the rotational-speed range, and the efficiency only becomes good at high rotational speed.

Part 3: Determine operating points

The function np.argmax(array) returns the index of the largest value in the array. With rpm[np.argmax(p_mech)] you therefore find the rotational speed at which the mechanical power is largest.

  1. Determine the rotational speed at which the mechanical power is maximal.

  2. Determine the rotational speed at which the efficiency is maximal.

  3. Compute the mean, standard deviation and maximum of the mechanical power over the entire run.

  4. Print the results as a short text report with f-strings.

# code cell
Solution Part 3
rpm_max_power = rpm[np.argmax(p_mech)]
rpm_max_efficiency = rpm[np.argmax(efficiency)]

mean_power = np.mean(p_mech)
power_spread = np.std(p_mech)
max_power = np.max(p_mech)

print(f"Largest mechanical power at {rpm_max_power} 1/min")
print(f"Best efficiency at {rpm_max_efficiency} 1/min")
print(f"Mechanical power: mean {mean_power:.0f} W, "
      f"spread {power_spread:.0f} W, maximum {max_power:.0f} W")

The mechanical power is largest at about 644 W at 1500 1/min, the efficiency at 0.84 only at 2000 1/min. The motor therefore does not deliver its highest power at the rotational speed at which it has the lowest losses.

Part 4: Efficiency with measurement uncertainty

Each efficiency value is the mean of three repeat measurements. The standard deviation of these repeats is given:

efficiency_std = np.array([0.015, 0.018, 0.020, 0.022, 0.025, 0.024,
                           0.022, 0.020, 0.019, 0.018, 0.028, 0.030])

  1. Plot the efficiency over the rotational speed with ax.errorbar() and error bars in the y-direction (fmt='o', capsize=4).

  2. Highlight the operating point with the best efficiency by drawing it with a second ax.scatter() call as a large star (marker='*', s=200).

  3. Label the axes and title and show a legend.

# code cell
Solution Part 4
efficiency_std = np.array([0.015, 0.018, 0.020, 0.022, 0.025, 0.024,
                           0.022, 0.020, 0.019, 0.018, 0.028, 0.030])

i_best = np.argmax(efficiency)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 4))

ax.errorbar(rpm, efficiency, yerr=efficiency_std,
            fmt='o', capsize=4, label='Measurement')
ax.scatter(rpm[i_best], efficiency[i_best],
           marker='*', s=200, color='red', zorder=5, label='Best operating point')

ax.set_xlabel('Rotational speed in 1/min')
ax.set_ylabel('Efficiency')
ax.set_title('Efficiency with measurement uncertainty')
ax.legend()
ax.grid(True)

plt.show()

The error bars are largest relative to the measured value at low rotational speed, because the efficiency itself is small there. The best operating point at 2000 1/min, on the other hand, is determined very precisely. The zorder=5 ensures that the star lies above the error bars.

Closing question

Answer in your own words, without further code:

  1. The motor delivers its largest mechanical power at 1500 1/min, but works most efficiently at 2000 1/min. For which rotational speed would you design the motor for continuous operation? Justify.

  2. At which measured points is the relative measurement uncertainty of the efficiency largest? What follows from this for planning further measurements?

Solution closing question

  1. For continuous operation, one usually chooses a rotational speed close to the best efficiency, that is, about 2000 1/min. There, the least energy is lost as heat, the motor runs cooler and the operating costs are lower. The maximum power at 1500 1/min is more of interest for short-term load peaks. If the application permanently needs the highest power, the lower efficiency must be deliberately accepted.

  2. The relative uncertainty is the quotient of efficiency_std and efficiency. It is largest at the low rotational speeds, because the efficiency itself is small there, while the absolute scatter stays similar. At 250 1/min it is over ten percent. For further measurements it is worth taking more repeats in the lower rotational-speed range or using a more accurate measurement method. The well-measured range around 2000 1/min, on the other hand, needs less effort.

Bonus exercise: Second test-rig run with a warm motor (✩✩✩)

After an hour of continuous running, the motor is warm. A second test-rig run at the same rotational speeds and the same torque delivers a higher power input, because the winding resistances rise with temperature:

power_input_warm = np.array([1420, 1780, 1880, 1680, 1370, 1100,
                             910, 830, 770, 710, 660, 560])   # W

  1. Compute the efficiency efficiency_warm for the warm run. The torque, and thus p_mech, stay unchanged.

  2. Plot both efficiency curves, cold and warm, in a common diagram, with a legend.

  3. Compare: how much does the peak efficiency drop? Does the rotational speed of the best operating point shift?

# code cell
Solution bonus exercise
power_input_warm = np.array([1420, 1780, 1880, 1680, 1370, 1100,
                             910, 830, 770, 710, 660, 560])

efficiency_warm = p_mech / power_input_warm

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 4))
ax.plot(rpm, efficiency,      marker='o', label='Cold motor')
ax.plot(rpm, efficiency_warm, marker='o', label='Warm motor')
ax.set_xlabel('Rotational speed in 1/min')
ax.set_ylabel('Efficiency')
ax.set_title('Efficiency with a cold and a warm motor')
ax.set_ylim(0, 1)
ax.legend()
ax.grid(True)
plt.show()

print(f"Peak efficiency cold: {np.max(efficiency):.2f} "
      f"at {rpm[np.argmax(efficiency)]} 1/min")
print(f"Peak efficiency warm: {np.max(efficiency_warm):.2f} "
      f"at {rpm[np.argmax(efficiency_warm)]} 1/min")

The peak efficiency drops from about 0.84 to about 0.73, that is, by more than ten percentage points. The rotational speed of the best operating point stays at 2000 1/min. The motor temperature therefore noticeably worsens the efficiency without shifting the location of the optimum. For a reliable report, the operating temperature at which the measurement was taken should always be stated.
