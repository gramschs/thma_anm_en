5.1 Rotation Matrices for a Robot Arm

In an assembly cell, a SCARA robot places electronic parts on circuit boards. SCARA stands for Selective Compliance Assembly Robot Arm: the arm gives a little in the horizontal direction but is stiff in the vertical direction. The robot consists of a base and two horizontal arms that rotate about vertical axes at two joints. The gripper at the end of the second arm can also move up and down. Many SCARA robots can rotate the gripper about a vertical axis as well. Where the gripper is in the horizontal plane, however, depends only on the angles of the two joints. The controller has to compute this position. Where does the gripper go when the first joint rotates by 30°?

In Chapters 3 and 4 we used matrices to set up and solve systems of equations. In this chapter, matrices take on a second role: a rotation matrix rotates points about the origin. We use it to compute where the gripper and the part it holds end up, and we check why the arm keeps its length as it rotates.

Learning objectives¶

Learning objectives

  • You can derive the rotation matrix R(φ)\mathbf{R}(\varphi)R(φ) from the rotated unit vectors and use it to rotate a point about the origin.

  • You can rotate many points stored as rows of an array in a single step with (R @ points.T).T and use this to compute the position and orientation of a part.

  • You know what an orthogonal matrix is and can undo a rotation with the transposed rotation matrix.

Where does the gripper go?¶

For the calculation we look at the robot from above. Both joints rotate about vertical axes, so the arms move only in the horizontal plane. The vertical stroke of the gripper only changes its height. For the position in the plane, the top view is therefore all we need.

We place the origin of the coordinate system at joint 1. Arm 1 is l1=0.4 ml_1 = 0.4\,\text{m}l1​=0.4m long, and arm 2 is l2=0.3 ml_2 = 0.3\,\text{m}l2​=0.3m long. We measure the angle α\alphaα of joint 1 from the xxx-axis and the angle β\betaβ of joint 2 from the extension of arm 1. In this chapter we keep joint 2 straight, i.e. β=0\beta = 0β=0. The whole arm then rotates about joint 1 like a rigid bar. In the home position α=0\alpha = 0α=0, the arm points along the xxx-axis. As with the node coordinates in Chapter 4.1, we store the points of the arm as rows of a 2D array.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

If joint 1 rotates by the angle α\alphaα, the gripper moves on a circle of radius 0.7 m0.7\,\text{m}0.7m around the origin. By the definition of cosine and sine on the circle, it ends up at (0.7cos⁡α, 0.7sin⁡α)(0.7\cos\alpha,\ 0.7\sin\alpha)(0.7cosα, 0.7sinα). This reasoning works because the gripper lies on the xxx-axis in the home position. The part in the gripper, however, has corners off the xxx-axis. We therefore need a formula that rotates any point (x,y)(x, y)(x,y).

To do this, we split the point into its components along the axes. We reach it by going xxx steps along the unit vector e⃗x=(1,0)⊤\vec{e}_x = (1, 0)^\topex​=(1,0)⊤ and yyy steps along e⃗y=(0,1)⊤\vec{e}_y = (0, 1)^\topey​=(0,1)⊤. Unit vectors have length 1, and these two point along the coordinate axes. When we rotate by the angle φ\varphiφ, both unit vectors rotate as well.

The figure shows that e⃗x\vec{e}_xex​ becomes (cos⁡φ,sin⁡φ)⊤(\cos\varphi, \sin\varphi)^\top(cosφ,sinφ)⊤ and e⃗y\vec{e}_yey​ becomes (−sin⁡φ,cos⁡φ)⊤(-\sin\varphi, \cos\varphi)^\top(−sinφ,cosφ)⊤. The rotated point is made up of the same components, but now along the rotated unit vectors:

p⃗ ′=x(cos⁡φsin⁡φ)+y(−sin⁡φcos⁡φ)=(xcos⁡φ−ysin⁡φxsin⁡φ+ycos⁡φ).\vec{p}^{\,\prime} = x \begin{pmatrix} \cos\varphi \\ \sin\varphi \end{pmatrix} + y \begin{pmatrix} -\sin\varphi \\ \cos\varphi \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} x\cos\varphi - y\sin\varphi \\ x\sin\varphi + y\cos\varphi \end{pmatrix}.p​′=x(cosφsinφ​)+y(−sinφcosφ​)=(xcosφ−ysinφxsinφ+ycosφ​).

The right-hand side is exactly what we get when we multiply a matrix by the vector (x,y)⊤(x, y)^\top(x,y)⊤. We computed this product row by row in Chapter 3.1:

p⃗ ′=(cos⁡φ−sin⁡φsin⁡φcos⁡φ)⏟R(φ)(xy).\vec{p}^{\,\prime} = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} \cos\varphi & -\sin\varphi \\ \sin\varphi & \cos\varphi \end{pmatrix}}_{\mathbf{R}(\varphi)} \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix}.p​′=R(φ)(cosφsinφ​−sinφcosφ​)​​(xy​).

The matrix R(φ)\mathbf{R}(\varphi)R(φ) is called the rotation matrix. Its columns are the rotated unit vectors. A positive angle rotates counterclockwise, a negative angle clockwise.

We test the formula on a case we can easily work out in our heads. For φ=90°\varphi = 90°φ=90°, we have cos⁡90°=0\cos 90° = 0cos90°=0 and sin⁡90°=1\sin 90° = 1sin90°=1. The gripper at (0.7,0)(0.7, 0)(0.7,0) then ends up at

R(90°)(0.70)=(0−110)(0.70)=(0⋅0.7+(−1)⋅01⋅0.7+0⋅0)=(00.7).\mathbf{R}(90°) \begin{pmatrix} 0.7 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0.7 \\ 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \cdot 0.7 + (-1) \cdot 0 \\ 1 \cdot 0.7 + 0 \cdot 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 \\ 0.7 \end{pmatrix}.R(90°)(0.70​)=(01​−10​)(0.70​)=(0⋅0.7+(−1)⋅01⋅0.7+0⋅0​)=(00.7​).

After a quarter turn, the arm therefore points along the yyy-axis, as expected.

Rotations in 3D space

The joints of the SCARA robot rotate about the vertical zzz-axis. In three dimensions, the rotation matrix for this is

Rz(φ)=(cos⁡φ−sin⁡φ0sin⁡φcos⁡φ0001).\mathbf{R}_z(\varphi) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\varphi & -\sin\varphi & 0 \\ \sin\varphi & \cos\varphi & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.Rz​(φ)=⎝⎛​cosφsinφ0​−sinφcosφ0​001​⎠⎞​.

The top-left block is our rotation matrix, and the zzz-coordinate stays the same. That is why the top view is enough for the SCARA robot.

In Python we write the rotation matrix as a function rotation_matrix(angle) that returns a 2×2 array. np.cos and np.sin expect the angle in radians, not in degrees. np.deg2rad does the conversion. We rotate the point with the matrix multiplication operator @ from Chapter 3.1.

def rotation_matrix(angle):
    """Rotation matrix for a rotation about the origin, angle in radians."""
    return np.array([
        [np.cos(angle), -np.sin(angle)],
        [np.sin(angle),  np.cos(angle)],
    ])

# joint 1 rotates by 30 degrees, np.deg2rad converts degrees to radians
alpha = np.deg2rad(30)
R = rotation_matrix(alpha)

# rotate the gripper: rotation matrix times point
gripper = arm[2]
gripper_new = R @ gripper

print('Rotation matrix R(30°):')
print(np.round(R, 4))
print('Gripper after the rotation in m:', np.round(gripper_new, 4))

After the rotation, the gripper is at (0.606,0.350)(0.606, 0.350)(0.606,0.350). This matches our reasoning on the circle: 0.7cos⁡30°=0.6060.7\cos 30° = 0.6060.7cos30°=0.606 and 0.7sin⁡30°=0.3500.7\sin 30° = 0.3500.7sin30°=0.350. The first column of the rotation matrix is (0.866,0.5)(0.866, 0.5)(0.866,0.5), i.e. the rotated unit vector e⃗x\vec{e}_xex​.

Angles in radians

The call np.cos(30) interprets 30 as radians, not degrees, and returns 0.154 instead of 0.866 without any error message. We therefore always convert degrees with np.deg2rad first.

Rotating the whole arm at once¶

So far we have rotated only the gripper. The controller, however, needs more points. From the position of joint 2 it can tell whether the arm collides with anything. From the corners of the part it can tell whether the part lands correctly on the circuit board. The part is 8 cm8\,\text{cm}8cm long and 4 cm4\,\text{cm}4cm wide. It is centered under the gripper and, in the home position, lies lengthwise along the arm.

Do we have to multiply each point by R\mathbf{R}R separately? No, we can do it in one step. The product R⋅p⃗\mathbf{R} \cdot \vec{p}R⋅p​ expects the point as a column with two entries. Our arrays, on the other hand, store each point as a row and have the shape (n, 2). The transpose .T from Chapter 4.2 swaps rows and columns. part.T has the shape (2, 4), and each column is a corner. R @ part.T multiplies R\mathbf{R}R by every column at once and again gives the shape (2, 4). A final .T converts the result back to one point per row.

# corners of the part in the gripper in m, home position, row = [x, y]
part = np.array([
    [0.66, -0.02],
    [0.74, -0.02],
    [0.74,  0.02],
    [0.66,  0.02],
])

# shapes before and after transposing
print('Shape of part:      ', part.shape)
print('Shape of part.T:    ', part.T.shape)
print('Shape of R @ part.T:', (R @ part.T).shape)
Shape of part:       (4, 2)
Shape of part.T:     (2, 4)
Shape of R @ part.T: (2, 4)
Remember: columns in the formula, rows in the array

In mathematics we write a point as a column, so in R⋅p⃗\mathbf{R} \cdot \vec{p}R⋅p​ the matrix is to the left of the point. In our NumPy arrays, on the other hand, each point is a row. The first .T turns the rows into columns, and the second .T turns the columns back into rows.

The rotation matrix R is still the rotation by 30° from the previous section. We use it to rotate the arm and the part and plot both positions.

# rotate all points at once: transpose, multiply, transpose back
arm_rotated = (R @ arm.T).T
part_rotated = (R @ part.T).T

print('Arm after the rotation by 30° in m:')
print(np.round(arm_rotated, 4))

plot_robot([arm, arm_rotated], parts=[part, part_rotated],
           labels=['Home position', 'α = 30°'],
           title='Joint 1 rotates by 30°')

[[0.     0.    ]

Joint 1 stays where it is, because a rotation about the origin does not move the origin. Joint 2 ends up at (0.346,0.200)(0.346, 0.200)(0.346,0.200), and the gripper, as before, at (0.606,0.350)(0.606, 0.350)(0.606,0.350). Both move on circles around joint 1. The part, however, does more than move along. It also rotates: its long edge is now tilted by 30° relative to the xxx-axis. A rotation therefore changes not only the position of a body but also its orientation, i.e. how it is aligned.

Why does the arm keep its length?¶

A rigid arm must neither stretch nor compress when it rotates. We first check whether our calculation respects this. As with the member length in Chapter 4.1, we compute the distance of the gripper from joint 1 with the Pythagorean theorem, x2+y2\sqrt{x^2 + y^2}x2+y2​.

# rotation by 30 degrees as in the previous section
R = rotation_matrix(np.deg2rad(30))
arm_rotated = (R @ arm.T).T

# distance of the gripper from joint 1 (Pythagorean theorem), before and after the rotation
distance_before = np.sqrt(arm[2, 0]**2 + arm[2, 1]**2)
distance_after = np.sqrt(arm_rotated[2, 0]**2 + arm_rotated[2, 1]**2)

print(f'Distance before: {distance_before:.4f} m')
print(f'Distance after:  {distance_after:.4f} m')
Distance before: 0.7000 m
Distance after:  0.7000 m

The distance is 0.7 m0.7\,\text{m}0.7m before and after the rotation. Is that due to the numbers we chose, or does it hold for every angle? The answer lies in the rotation matrix itself. We multiply its transpose by R\mathbf{R}R:

R⊤R=(cos⁡φsin⁡φ−sin⁡φcos⁡φ)(cos⁡φ−sin⁡φsin⁡φcos⁡φ)=(cos⁡2φ+sin⁡2φ00sin⁡2φ+cos⁡2φ)=(1001).\mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\varphi & \sin\varphi \\ -\sin\varphi & \cos\varphi \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \cos\varphi & -\sin\varphi \\ \sin\varphi & \cos\varphi \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos^2\varphi + \sin^2\varphi & 0 \\ 0 & \sin^2\varphi + \cos^2\varphi \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.R⊤R=(cosφ−sinφ​sinφcosφ​)(cosφsinφ​−sinφcosφ​)=(cos2φ+sin2φ0​0sin2φ+cos2φ​)=(10​01​).

Off the diagonal, the terms cancel, for example in the top right: cos⁡φ⋅(−sin⁡φ)+sin⁡φ⋅cos⁡φ=0\cos\varphi \cdot (-\sin\varphi) + \sin\varphi \cdot \cos\varphi = 0cosφ⋅(−sinφ)+sinφ⋅cosφ=0. On the diagonal we get cos⁡2φ+sin⁡2φ=1\cos^2\varphi + \sin^2\varphi = 1cos2φ+sin2φ=1, which is the Pythagorean theorem on the unit circle. The result is the identity matrix I\mathbf{I}I, with ones on the diagonal and zeros everywhere else. A matrix with R⊤R=I\mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}R⊤R=I is called an orthogonal matrix. Orthogonal matrices change neither lengths nor angles. That is why the arm and the part keep their shape, whatever the angle of rotation.

In Python, np.eye(2) creates the 2×2 identity matrix. We compare the result with np.allclose from Chapter 3.1, because cosine and sine involve small rounding errors. In addition, we compute the determinant with np.linalg.det.

# R transposed times R should give the identity matrix
product = R.T @ R
print(np.round(product, 4))
print('R^T R equals identity matrix:', np.allclose(product, np.eye(2)))

# determinant of the rotation matrix
print(f'Determinant of R: {np.linalg.det(R):.4f}')
[[1. 0.]
 [0. 1.]]
R^T R equals identity matrix: True
Determinant of R: 1.0000

The calculation confirms R⊤R=I\mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}R⊤R=I. The determinant is 1. It is therefore nonzero, so, as we saw in Chapter 3.1, the rotation can be uniquely reversed. The equation R⊤R=I\mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}R⊤R=I tells us directly how: the transpose undoes the rotation. A matrix that undoes the effect of another matrix is called its inverse matrix R−1\mathbf{R}^{-1}R−1. For rotation matrices, the inverse is simply the transpose, R−1=R⊤\mathbf{R}^{-1} = \mathbf{R}^\topR−1=R⊤. For the robot this means: if we multiply the rotated arm by R⊤\mathbf{R}^\topR⊤, it moves back to the home position.

# back to the home position: rotate with the transposed rotation matrix
arm_back = (R.T @ arm_rotated.T).T

print(np.round(arm_back, 4))
print('Home position reached:', np.allclose(arm_back, arm))
[[ 0.   0. ]
 [ 0.4 -0. ]
 [ 0.7 -0. ]]
Home position reached: True

The arm is back in the home position. The output shows -0. in some places. These are tiny negative rounding errors on the order of 10-18, which np.round displays as -0. Here, too, we therefore compare with np.allclose and not with ==.

Summary and outlook¶

A rotation matrix R(φ)\mathbf{R}(\varphi)R(φ) rotates points about the origin. Its columns are the rotated unit vectors, and a positive angle rotates counterclockwise. If we store many points as rows of an array, we rotate all of them in one step with (R @ points.T).T. Because R⊤R=I\mathbf{R}^\top \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}R⊤R=I, the rotation matrix is orthogonal: it preserves lengths and angles, and its transpose undoes the rotation. For our SCARA robot this means that we can compute the position of the arm, the gripper and the part from the angle of joint 1.

In Chapter 5.2, we apply the rotation matrix in a project: a swivel arm moves parts from a conveyor belt into a box. So far, joint 2 of our SCARA robot has stayed straight. If it rotates as well, its axis of rotation is no longer at the origin but at the end of arm 1. A rotation matrix alone is then not enough. Chapter 5.3 shows how to combine rotation and translation in a single matrix using homogeneous coordinates.
